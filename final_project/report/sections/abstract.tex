\begin{abstract}
Quantization stores the weights of a large language model (LLM) in 8 or 4 bits instead of 16, and
formats are usually compared by this bit-width. We show that the bit-width alone predicts neither the
memory a format saves, nor its speed, nor its cost in quality. On one NVIDIA RTX 5090 we measure eight
configurations on two inference stacks (Hugging Face Transformers with bitsandbytes, and vLLM) for five
models from 3B to 32B parameters, with \NumMeasurements{} timed runs, real chat traffic and three
quality benchmarks. The results form a chain. First, 4-bit weights shrink a 7B model only
\WtNFSeven--\WtAWQSeven, not $4\times$, because the embedding, the KV cache and the activations stay
in 16 bits. Second, the saved bytes become speed only when decoding is memory-bound and a fused kernel
hides the dequantization: at batch size~1, 4-bit weights decode \AWQspeedup{} faster on vLLM but only
\NFsevenB{} faster with bitsandbytes. Third, batching makes decoding compute-bound, so these
weight-only gains vanish, as a roofline model predicts; only FP8, whose fused kernel also does
the multiply in 8 bits, stays faster at every batch size. Fourth, the gains cost little quality at 8 bits and
\MFourAbsMin--\MFourAbsMax{} MMLU points at 4 bits, more on smaller models. The best use of the saved
memory is therefore a larger model: Qwen3-32B in 4 bits scores \BigMMLUawq\% on MMLU against
\SmallMMLUbf\% for Qwen3-8B in BF16, with only \BigMemRatio{} the weight memory.
\end{abstract}
